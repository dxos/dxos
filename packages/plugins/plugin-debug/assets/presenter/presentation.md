# The Great Man Fallacy

> “The History of the world is but the Biography of great men.”

[Thomas Carlyle]

<!--
- Carlyle: history is driven by exceptional individuals.
- The fallacy credits or blames individuals for outcomes that systems produce.
- Technology and institutions create the conditions that make particular leaders possible.
- Applied to AI, a focus on founders and CEOs hides the incentives that drive the race.

Notes
- Attaches undo weight to individuals.
- The central debate is about causation: Did Napoleon transform Europe because of his exceptional abilities,
  or did revolutionary France create conditions in which someone like Napoleon would rise?
- Other approaches emphasize institutions, economics, technology, social movements, and ordinary people.
- A balanced interpretation is that circumstances shape the opportunities available, while individual decisions can strongly influence what happens next.
-->

# The Molloch

The Coordination Trap Behind the Metacrisis

- Rational individual choices can produce collective disaster.
- Competition rewards speed, growth, and power over safety.
- Those who exercise restraint risk losing to those who don’t.
- No actor can safely change course alone.
- Hidden costs accumulate: ecological damage, distrust, and instability.
- Interlocking crises overwhelm our capacity to coordinate.
- Powerful technologies accelerate these destructive dynamics.
- Escape requires changing incentives so cooperation can succeed.

<!--
- Moloch, from Ginsberg’s “Howl”, as Scott Alexander used it: the personification of coordination failure.
- The metacrisis is not a set of separate problems but one failure of incentives.
- It sets up the choice on the next slides: control (blue pill) or reform (red pill).

- Tragedy of the commons (economic externalities)
- Moloch names a destructive competitive dynamic: people and institutions can collectively create outcomes that almost nobody wants, even while each acts rationally within the system.
- Competition rewards harmful choices: a company that sacrifices safety for speed may outperform a cautious rival, pushing others to follow.
- Individual restraint can become a disadvantage: refusing to pollute, exploit workers, or escalate an arms race may mean losing influence to actors who will.
- These dynamics create “multipolar traps”: many competing actors become locked into behavior that none can safely abandon alone.
- The costs accumulate outside the competition: environmental damage, distrust, burnout, and systemic risk grow while participants pursue narrower measures of success.
- Technology raises the stakes: increasingly powerful tools let competitive pressures produce damage faster and at greater scale, potentially exceeding society’s ability to respond.
- The metacrisis is the interaction of these failures: ecological, political, economic, and technological crises reinforce one another through shared incentives and weakened coordination.
- Escaping requires changing the incentives: enforceable agreements, trustworthy institutions, and collective limits must make cooperation viable; asking individuals to behave better is insufficient on its own.
-->
